✈️ How Wings Lift an Aircraft

Explore how wing angle changes lift and the way an aircraft flies.

🎛️ Wing Control

Small angle (0°–4°) → Low lift
Increasing angle (5°–8°) → Lift increases
Best-lift range (9°–12°) → High lift
Near critical angle (13°–15°) → Lift begins to decrease
Beyond critical angle (16°+) → Stall; lift drops significantly

💡 Note: This is a simplified model. The exact stall angle depends on the aircraft and wing design.

💨 Airflow Visualization

“Changing the wing angle changes how air flows around the wing and affects lift.”

The moving arrows represent airflow. A moderate wing angle can produce more lift, while a very large angle can cause a stall.

📏 The dashed yellow line shows the direction of the incoming airflow. The wing tilts relative to that line — that tilt is the angle of attack, not the angle of the aircraft's nose.

💨 Bernoulli's principle: watch the small arrows right above and below the wing — as you raise the wing angle, the stream above the wing speeds up while the one below stays slower. Faster-moving air above the wing means lower pressure there, which helps pull the wing upward. Push the angle too far and that fast stream above the wing breaks apart into a choppy, turbulent flow — that's the stall, where lift collapses.

🧠 Simple Idea

⬆️ Lift greater than weight → aircraft rises.
➡️ Lift about equal to weight → aircraft maintains height.
⬇️ Lift too low → aircraft descends.
🔼 Lift rises steadily as angle of attack increases, peaking in the 9°–12° best-lift range.
⚠️ Past 12° (13°–15°), lift starts falling even as angle keeps increasing — airflow is struggling to stay attached.
⚠️ Beyond the critical angle (16°+) → stall: airflow separates and lift collapses.
